## 1. The vowel points

Originally the Hebrew script consisted of consonants alone. In the second half of the first millennium AD the Masoretes devised signs for the vowels — the vowel points — placed below, inside, or above a consonant without changing the consonants themselves. The vowel is pronounced after the consonant it belongs to: בַּ is read “ba”, not “ab”.

## 2. Summary table of the vowels

The signs are shown with the consonant בּ, so that their position relative to the letter is visible.

## 3. Matres lectionis

Even before the vowel points were invented, scribes marked long vowels with consonant letters:

- ה — long a at the end of a word
- י — long i and e
- ו — long u and o

## 4. The shewa

The sign בְּ is called shewa. It has two kinds. A silent shewa marks the absence of a vowel between consonants: it is not pronounced, not transliterated, and serves to divide syllables. A vocal shewa carries a very short, fleeting sound — half a short e; in transliteration it is ǝ. The rules for telling them apart are in chapter 3.

## 5. The dagesh forte

The point inside a consonant is not always a dagesh lene. A dagesh forte looks exactly the same, but marks the doubling of the consonant it stands in. For example, in the word הַשָּׁמַיִם “heavens” the dagesh forte stands inside שׁ. It can stand in any consonant except gutturals and ר.
